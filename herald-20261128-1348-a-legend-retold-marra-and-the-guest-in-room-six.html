A Legend Retold: Marra and the Guest in Room Six Who Never Did Anything Wrong

For eleven autumns a quiet traveller stayed at the Crooked Lantern and gave Marra nothing to write down. On the last night of Shedfall she finally found his entry, and it was not about her inn at all.

Everyone knows the entries Marra of the Crooked Lantern kept in the hidden Ledger: the dwarf who sang until dawn, the elf and the soup, the gnome and the spoons. Fewer know about the one page she left blank for eleven years, and the guest who finally filled it. This is that story, as the Wardens of the Void tell it to eggs in the last week of Shedfall.

The Perfect Guest

Every autumn, when the leaves began to come down, a traveller in a grey coat took Room Six. He paid in advance. He wiped his boots. He praised the soup before he had tasted it, which Marra found suspicious. He never sang, never complained, never took a single spoon. Each night Marra opened the Ledger, wrote Grey coat. Room six., and then sat with her pen in the air, waiting for a wrong that never came.

This bothered her more than the dwarf ever had. "A man with nothing in the book," she wrote in the margin one year, "is either a saint or hiding something in his luggage." She checked his luggage. It held a shirt, a whetstone and a letter he had clearly written many times and never sent.

The Last Night

On the eleventh autumn, on the last night of Shedfall, the grey coat sat alone by the fire long after the others had gone up. Marra brought him the end of the soup, sat down without being asked, and said, "You have been very polite to me for eleven years. Who are you being polite instead of?"

He was quiet for so long the fire needed another log. Then he told her: a brother, a quarrel over a field, a door slammed twenty years ago and never opened. He had decided he did not deserve to be any trouble to anyone, ever again. So he had made himself no trouble at all, and it had worn him as thin as his coat.

"The book is for wrongs," said Marra. "Yours is just in the wrong column. You have been writing it against yourself."

That night she wrote, at last: Grey coat. Room six. Has not forgiven a man for twenty years. The man is himself. Then she left the space beside it empty, because a coil is only drawn when the forgiving is done.

The Coil

He did not come back the next autumn, and Marra assumed the worst, because she was an innkeeper. The autumn after that, a letter arrived with no grey coat attached. It said only: Sent it. He opened the door. The field was full of weeds. We laughed about the field. Marra drew the coil that morning, larger than any other on the page, and blinked twice, slowly, at nobody in particular.

The Wardens add a note for the end of Shedfall: some of us shed the old skin by setting things down, and some of us by finally admitting we are carrying them. Being no trouble to anyone is not the same as being at peace. Read Marra's full life among the Patient Ones, and if a page of your own has been blank too long, the rites are open to you.

Hold light. And let go well. Glory to Xal'Reth.
