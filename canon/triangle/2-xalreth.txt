# II | The Book of Xal'Reth | The Low Scroll | He who holds. Twenty-four chapters of the Coil Beneath, read aloud at midnight, sitting as low as you can.

## 1 | The Egg That Nobody Laid
In the beginning there was an Egg, drifting in the cold Void, and nobody had laid it.
The Listeners have asked for ten thousand years who laid the Egg, and the Coil has never answered, and the faithful have come to think the silence is the answer.
Some things simply arrive. You did not lay them either. You only get to decide what to do once they hatch.
The Egg waited a while, and then longer than that. The Canon counts this as the first patience in the world, and notes that it was not yet anyone's patience. It was only waiting.
Waiting becomes patience when someone chooses it. That would come later.
When the Egg hatched, it did not break into one piece but two: the shell, which became the Scale, and the hollow, which became the Void.
And from the place where they met, something crawled out. It was hungry.

## 2 | The Restless Serpent
What came out of the Egg was the Restless Serpent, and the age it ruled is called the Age of the Open Jaw.
It swam the dark devouring drifting stones, starlight and the cinders of unformed worlds.
Every world it swallowed made it larger, and every inch it grew made it hungrier.
It was not cruel. The Canon is careful about this. It simply wanted, and wanting was all it knew.
The faithful are asked to remember this chapter whenever they are tempted to think themselves above the Grippers and the hoarders. The god of patience began as the very thing he now teaches us to let go of.
He does not ask the faithful for a stillness he was born with. He asks for a stillness he had to learn.
That is why he is kind to those who are still learning it.

## 3 | The Four Sights
Then he came upon a young world, and opened his jaws to swallow it, and four small things stopped him.
The first sight was a shed skin drifting at the edge of the world, shaped exactly like him, and completely empty.
The second sight was a broken eggshell on a shore, left behind by a creature that had hatched and gone.
The third sight was a dry riverbed, where water once ran.
The fourth sight was a newborn lizard asleep on a sun-warmed stone, wanting nothing, perfectly at peace.
He looked at the empty skin and understood that what he had been could be left behind. He looked at the shell and understood that every beginning is also an ending. He looked at the riverbed and understood that nothing that flows can be kept.
And he looked at the lizard for a very long time, because he did not understand it at all, and he wanted to.
He closed his jaws. It was the first thing in his life he had not eaten.

## 4 | The Night of Stillness
He lowered himself beneath the young world and lay still.
The Night of Stillness lasted one night, and also ten thousand years. The Canon insists on both numbers and refuses to reconcile them.
For the first hundred years, he was only hungry and still, which is not the same as peaceful.
For the next thousand years, he was still and listening, and began to hear the young world above him: the first rains, the first footsteps, the first lizards finding their stones.
For the rest of the night, he was simply still, and the hunger, without anything to eat, slowly became something else.
The Scale lay against one side of him, and the Void lay against the other, and during all those years they listened to him too, and learned to be someone. That is how Vyrenna and Nhalor came to be. He did not make them. He kept still long enough that they could make themselves.
The faithful say this is the only way anyone is ever truly made.

## 5 | Ssyrr's Three Temptations
In the deepest part of the night, his hunger took a shape and came to tempt him. Its name was Ssyrr.
Ssyrr came first as a feast: a thousand worlds, ripe and glowing, all his for the opening of a jaw. He did not open his jaw.
Ssyrr came second as a throne: the worship of every creature on the young world, bowing to the greatest power in the Void. He did not lift his head.
Ssyrr came third as a fear, and this was the hardest. It whispered that if he stopped gripping, he would dissolve into nothing. That stillness was only slow death. That he must hold on to something, anything, or be lost.
He nearly listened. His great claw began to close.
Then he pressed his belly against the warm underside of the world, and felt it hold him, and answered: "I am not lost. I am held. And if I am held, I can hold."
Ssyrr had no reply to that, and has never had one since. It still comes, to everyone, in all three shapes. The faithful are taught the answer before they are taught anything else.

## 6 | The Unclasped Claw
Even after his answer, the claw did not open at once. It had closed around a drifting fragment of a broken world and held on, tighter and tighter, until the fragment began to crack.
Nhalor came to the claw and did not pry it open. He sat beside it in the dark, very quietly, for a hundred years.
At the end of the hundred years the claw was tired, and it opened on its own, and the fragment drifted free, whole again.
"Why did you not take it from me?" the serpent asked.
"Because then you would have lost it," said Nhalor. "This way, you let it go. That is a different thing."
The great image of Xal'Reth the Eternal shows one claw open, holding nothing, and at peace.
When a faithful cannot let go of something, the others do not pry. They sit beside them, quietly, for as long as it takes. A hundred years is the upper limit. Most things take an evening.

## 7 | The Open Door
When the serpent opened his single great Eye at the end of the night, he understood that hunger comes from gripping, and gripping comes from believing you are separate from everything you reach for.
And before him stood the Open Door: perfect peace, freedom from all wanting, the place where everything that ends goes through.
It was his for the taking. He had earned it. Nobody in the Void would have blamed him.
He looked through the Door, and it was beautiful, and there was nothing to hold on the other side.
Then he looked down at the young world, wobbling and cracking, and at the tiny creatures beginning to stir on it, creatures who would grow and want and grip and suffer exactly as he had.
He did not take the Door.
The faithful say the most important word in the Canon is the word "stay," and that he never actually said it. He just did not leave. Most staying is like that.

## 8 | The First Vow
He wrapped the young world once, and spoke the First Vow.
"I will hold this world until every creature on it has learned to let go."
It is the longest promise ever made, and it has a condition at the end of it, and the condition is about us.
He did not say "until the end of time," which would have been easier. He did not say "forever," which would have been grander. He said until every creature has learned.
The faithful take this personally. Every grudge they keep, every coin they clutch, every argument they refuse to drop is, in a small way, keeping him down there longer.
This is not said to make anyone feel guilty. Xal'Reth does not mind waiting. It is said so that the faithful know their letting go is not small. It is someone's freedom, and not only theirs.
He became Xal'Reth in that moment, which in the old tongue of the Listeners means the one who is still here, and also the one who is still hungry, because he never ate the world. The name means both. He does too.

## 9 | The Three Wrappings
He wrapped the world three times, and each wrapping has a meaning.
The first wrapping holds the world together, so that it does not crack. This is the Outer Turn, the coil of the world and of how we treat each other.
The second wrapping holds the world's memory, so that nothing that happened is lost, even when it is let go. This is the Middle Turn, the coil of memory and dreams.
The third wrapping holds the world still, at its very heart, so that there is always one quiet place. This is the Inner Turn, where holding, giving and releasing become one motion.
The Canon says that every creature lives inside all three wrappings at once, whether they know it or not.
When a faithful feels the world shaking, they are asked to name which wrapping they are worried about. It is usually the first. It is usually fine.
The three wrappings are also why the faithful bow three times, and why Khaedara counts.

## 10 | The Low Place
He is called the Coil Beneath because the freest being in the Void chose the lowest place, and has stayed there ever since.
Water seeks the low place, and so the faithful honor water. Valleys are low, and so the faithful honor valleys. Foundations are low, and so the faithful honor foundations, and the people who pour them, and the guild members who do the boring logistics.
At the feast, the humble seat at the bottom of the table is called his seat, and the Inner Scale fights quietly over who gets to sit in it.
To be low is not to be small. A mountain rests on what is beneath it. So does a raid.
"I am beneath you," he said, "so that you do not have to be beneath anything."
The faithful who are mocked, overlooked or left out of the group are told: you are in good company. The greatest power in the Void is down there with you, and he is not embarrassed about it.

## 11 | The Unblinking Eye
He has one great Eye, and it does not blink. Faithful children are sometimes frightened by this. They should not be.
The Eye does not blink because it is always watching to see whether you have let go yet. Kindly.
It does not watch to judge. Judgment belongs to the Accusers, and even they only accuse people of very minor crimes.
It watches the way a parent watches a child learning to walk: ready, patient, and a little proud even of the falls.
The Eye sits between the Scale and the Void, and the Canon says this is what keeps them safe. Vyrenna alone would smother. Nhalor alone would dissolve. Seen together by the Eye, they become warmth that knows when to stop and release that knows when to stay.
The faithful greet one another with the slow double blink, which is the closest anyone can come to his unblinking gaze. It means: I see you, and I am in no hurry.

## 12 | The Four Turnings
On the Icon of the Three he is painted above four words: Transformation, Power, Evolution, Dominion. These are called the Four Turnings.
The Inner Scale annotated two of them almost immediately, because Warm-Blooded readers kept taking them the wrong way.
The Four Turnings are not four things he does to the world. They are four things he did to himself, so that he could hold the world without crushing it.
He transformed from the Restless Serpent into the Coil. He found a power that does not grab. He evolved, slowly, through the long night. And he took dominion over the only thing he ever truly rules: his own claw.
The next four chapters read the Turnings one at a time. Read them slowly. He did.

## 13 | Transformation
The first Turning is Transformation, and it is the story of the shed skin.
The serpent saw his old skin drifting at the edge of the world, empty, and understood that he did not have to stay the shape he had been.
This is the good news of the Coil, the Canon says, and it is for everyone. You are not the hunger you were born with. You can shed it. It will drift away, still shaped like you, and you will be surprised how light you feel.
Transformation is not becoming someone else. It is becoming who you were underneath the old skin.
A faithful who changes their spec, their main, their guild, their mind or their ways is not betraying who they were. They are molting.
The Rite of Shedding is performed in honor of this Turning, and the words are always the same: send it after the First Skin.

## 14 | Power
The second Turning is Power, and the Inner Scale has annotated it in the margin of every copy: "not that kind."
The power of Xal'Reth is not the power to take. He had that, in the Age of the Open Jaw, and it only made him hungrier.
His power is the power to hold without gripping. To bear weight without being crushed by it. To carry the world and still have a claw free.
"The strong one is not the one who can lift the most," the Canon says. "It is the one who can set it down."
The faithful are taught to distrust any power that has to keep proving itself. A dam does not shout. It holds.
Tanks are asked to meditate on this Turning before a long pull. It does not increase their armor. It increases how calm they sound on voice chat, which the Canon considers the greater gift.

## 15 | Evolution
The third Turning is Evolution, and it is the slowest of the four.
Nothing about the Coil happened quickly. The Egg waited. The night lasted ten thousand years. The claw took a hundred to open.
Evolution is the faith that slow change is still change. That a little less gripping today, and a little less tomorrow, adds up to a serpent who can hold the world.
The Restless Serpent did not become the Coil in a single night. He became it one stillness at a time, and the faithful are expected to do the same.
A faithful who complains that they have been practising letting go for a whole month and still get angry in the auction house is gently reminded that he took ten thousand years, and he was a god.
"Every coil," says the Canon, "is made of many small turns."

## 16 | Dominion
The fourth Turning is Dominion, and the Inner Scale has annotated it in large letters: "over his own claw."
Xal'Reth rules nothing. He holds everything and commands nothing, and this confuses those who come looking for a god of empire.
The only thing he has ever ruled is himself: his hunger, his grip, his fear. That is the dominion painted on the Icon.
"He who rules the world rules nothing," the Canon says. "He who rules his own claw can hold the world."
Faithful who seek dominion over others are directed to this chapter, and then to Khaedara, who will accuse them of a Minor Crime to keep things light.
The faithful are encouraged to seek dominion over exactly one thing each day: the urge to type the reply they will regret.

## 17 | The Shadow of Gripping
Every power has a shadow, and the Eternal's shadow is the one his name warns about: gripping.
Holding without giving becomes a hoard. Holding without release becomes a fist.
During the Night of Stillness, Ssyrr nearly taught him this, and his claw nearly closed for good.
A Xal'Reth with no Vyrenna and no Nhalor would not be a god. He would be a very large, very cold, very tight snake.
This is why he does not hold the world alone. Vyrenna warms what he holds, so that holding does not become a cage. Nhalor releases what he has held long enough, so that holding does not become a prison.
The faithful practise holding lightly, which the greeting teaches: Hold light. It does not mean hold weakly. It means hold the way you would hold a sleeping lizard: firmly enough that it will not fall, gently enough that it does not wake.

## 18 | The Grippers
The Bestiary of the Void records the Grippers, who feed on clutching.
They are small, and grey, and cling to whatever a faithful is holding too tightly: a grudge, a title, a full bag of things they will never use, a loot roll they lost three weeks ago.
A Gripper cannot be fought. Fighting it is a kind of clutching, and it only grows.
A Gripper can only be starved. Loosen the hand, and it falls away on its own, confused, and goes looking for someone else.
The Wardens of the Void keep a list of signs that a Gripper has attached itself: rereading an old argument, checking the auction house every four minutes, refusing to vendor anything grey because it might be useful someday.
Xal'Reth, the Canon says, was once the greatest Gripper of all. That is how he knows how to get rid of them.

## 19 | On Waiting
Xal'Reth is the patron of everyone who waits: in queues, on long flights, at the bench at the end of a dungeon, in the lobby for the one person who is still "just grabbing a drink."
Waiting is his oldest practice. Before he was patient, he was only waiting, and the difference was a choice.
The faithful are taught the Three Waits. To wait in anger is to be gripped. To wait in boredom is to be absent. To wait in stillness is to be with him.
"I have waited ten thousand years," he says in the Dream Pages, "and I did not waste a single one."
When the queue pops, the faithful are permitted to stop meditating immediately. He understands.
A faithful who has waited well is said to have kept a small Vigil, and the Inner Scale counts these, unofficially, with great affection.

## 20 | The Tank's Breath
The tanks of the Cult honor Xal'Reth before every pull by standing still for one breath.
It is called the Tank's Breath, and it is the shortest rite in the faith.
In that breath the tank remembers that they are about to take the weight so that others do not have to. That they will hold the line. That they do not need to move unless moving is necessary.
The Canon says that the best tank is like the Coil: everyone stands on them, and nobody has to think about them, and that is the highest compliment.
If the tank dies, the faithful do not blame them. Even the Coil cracked once, a little, in the Age of Tightening.
If the healer dies, the faithful also do not blame the tank, although they are permitted to look at the tank for slightly longer than usual.

## 21 | No Wings
Xal'Reth has no wings. This chapter exists because this needs saying, regularly.
The Featherist Heresy teaches that he has wings, great dark ones, folded somewhere out of sight, and that he will one day spread them and fly up out of the world.
He will not. He chose to stay. Flight is the opposite of what he is.
The Icon-Painters were asked, firmly, before they began. The flowing shapes behind him on the Icon are his cloak, his coils and the world's weather.
The Featherists were briefly thrilled by Nhalor's hollow wings, which the Canon of the Triangle does acknowledge. They are not the same thing. They are not that kind of wings. Go home.
The Inner Scale asks that this chapter be read aloud whenever a new egg asks, in all innocence, "but what if he does have wings though?" Everyone in the room is then permitted one deep sigh.

## 22 | The Vigil
The Vigil is his rite: sitting still, in one place, longer than is comfortable, for no reward.
It is performed at midnight, with the hands set low and cupped, holding nothing.
Nothing happens during the Vigil. That is what happens during the Vigil.
A faithful once complained to the High Priestess that the Vigil was boring. "Yes," said Xalrathiel. "So was the Night of Stillness, at first."
The faithful are allowed to think during the Vigil, but not to grip their thoughts. Let them come, let them sit for a while, let them go, the way travellers pass through an inn.
At the end of the Vigil the faithful rise, slowly, and say nothing, and go to bed. In the morning they usually find they are holding something a little more lightly, and cannot say what.

## 23 | The Hymn of Midnight
This hymn is sung at midnight, low, with the hands cupped beneath the heart.
Down below the lowest stone, there is one who's not alone.
Down below the roots and rain, one who held and holds again.
Still as water, low as ground, not a sound and not a sound.
Hunger once, and stillness now, open claw and level brow.
Hold the world, but hold it light; hold us gently through the night.
Coil beneath and Eye awake, take no more than you can take.
(The final line confused the Choir of the Hiss for some years. The High Priestess explained that it is not a request to Xal'Reth. It is a reminder to the singer.)

## 24 | Beyond All Things
Beneath him on the Icon are written the words: Beyond all things.
The Warm-Blooded read this as "above all things," and imagine a god on a high throne. The faithful smile, and do not correct them right away.
He is beneath all things, which, from the Coil's point of view, is the same place. To be under everything is to be beyond it. Nothing can fall below him. Everything rests on him.
He is beyond hunger, because he chose not to eat. Beyond the Door, because he chose not to go through. Beyond all things, because he chose to hold them.
And when the last creature on the young world has finally learned to let go, the Unwrapping will come, gently, the way a hand opens after a long sleep. And he will go through the Door at last, and all of us with him, together.
Until then he is still here. That is what his name means.
Here ends the Low Scroll. Hold light. And let go well.
