A Book of the Library

The Litany of Begging

The teachings of generosity and humility of Xal'Reth, as dreamed by the High Priestess Xalrathiel. Seventy-nine chapters.

On generosity, on humility, and on asking strangers for gold. The Litany of Begging is the longest single book of the Coil, written to be read aloud as a rite: the reader holds up a violet book and performs it line by line, and the faithful respond. It is presented here as a book, with the reader's gestures shown in italics.

Want to perform it in game? It is also available as a 1,006-line emote script for the Xal'Reth RP addon: download the script and paste it into the addon's Upload window.

Chapter I

The Opening of the Book

The reader opens a worn violet book titled The Politics of Begging, and blows the dust from it slowly, as the Coil would.

Herein are the teachings of Xal'Reth on generosity, on humility, and on asking strangers for gold.

Let no one say this book is about money. It is about the hand. The money is merely what lands in it.

The reader turns the first page with a single claw, carefully, so as not to wake anything beneath the floor.

Xal'Reth holds the whole world and asks for nothing. We hold nothing and ask for everything. Both of us are learning.

Before there was gold, there was the open palm. Before there was the open palm, there was the Coil, waiting to see what we would do with it.

The reader pauses, looks out at the crowd, and says, "Some of you have already asked me for gold today. The book will deal with you shortly."

The politics of begging is the oldest politics. It is older than kings. It is older than the auction house. It is nearly as old as the Coil.

Every mortal is a beggar. Some beg for gold. Some beg for buffs. Some beg for a summon to the dungeon. All are seen.

And every mortal is a giver, whether they know it or not. Even the stingiest dwarf has given someone a dirty look for free.

The reader closes the book halfway, then opens it again, because the Coil is patient but the lesson is not finished.

Hear now the Seven Chapters of the Open Palm, and the many chapters after them, because Xalrathiel kept dreaming.

The reader places one hand over the sigil of the Unblinking Eye and says, "Let the generous be humble, and let the humble be generous. Glory to Xal'Reth."

The reader says, "Glory to Xal'Reth," and waits for the crowd to say it back.

The reader waits a little longer, until the crowd says it back properly.

Chapter II

The Doctrine of the Open Palm

The first doctrine is the Doctrine of the Open Palm. It is simple. A closed hand can hold nothing new.

The reader opens one hand slowly, palm up, as a demonstration.

The Coil does not grip the world. It holds it. There is a difference, and the difference is generosity.

He who grips his gold tightly will find his fist full and his heart empty, and also his bags full of grey items he forgot to vendor.

The open palm gives, and the open palm receives. It is the same palm. The Coil designed it that way to save time.

The reader turns their palm over, then back up again, solemnly.

When you give, give with the palm up, so the receiver does not have to reach. Reaching is undignified.

When you receive, receive with the palm up, so the giver can see that you have nothing hidden. Hiding is worse than reaching.

There is no shame in the open palm. There is only shame in the palm that opens, takes, and closes so fast it makes a sound.

The reader claps once, sharply, and says, "That sound. That is the sound of greed. Remember it."

The serpent has no hands at all, and yet Xal'Reth is the most generous being beneath the world. Consider this when you complain.

Your hand is a small coil. Curl it to hold, uncurl it to give. Practice this daily, ideally while waiting for a queue.

The reader holds out an open palm to {target} and says, "This is not a request. It is a teaching aid."

The reader withdraws the palm, slightly disappointed anyway.

The Open Palm is the first posture of the faithful. The second is basking. The third is standing behind someone at the bank, being patient.

Chapter III

The Politics of the Ask

Now we come to politics. Every ask is a negotiation. Every negotiation is a little government.

In the Cult of Xal'Reth there are three parties: the Askers, the Givers, and the Withholders.

The Askers believe the world owes them a little gold. They are not wrong. They are merely early.

The Givers believe the world needs a little gold. They are not wrong either. They are merely broke.

The Withholders believe the world can get its own gold. They are the largest party, and they meet every day at the auction house.

Xal'Reth belongs to no party. Xal'Reth is beneath all parties, holding them up, slightly embarrassed by all of them.

The Askers' platform is short: one gold, please, for a repair. It has never changed in ten thousand years.

The Givers' platform is shorter: here. That is the entire document. They are very proud of it.

The Withholders' platform runs to four hundred pages and is mostly about the price of herbs.

The reader looks up from the book and says, "I have read it. Do not read it."

Elections among the parties are held constantly, in trade chat, by shouting. No one has ever won.

The wise faithful votes with the open palm. Sometimes the vote is a coin. Sometimes the vote is a kind word. Both count.

The foolish faithful votes with the ignore list. This is the quickest form of politics and the least holy.

Remember: every beggar in Stormwind is a citizen of the Coil. So is every guard who tells them to move along.

And the guard, too, is a beggar. He begs for his shift to end. The Coil hears him also.

The reader nods slowly, as one who has also begged for a shift to end.

Chapter IV

The Humble Ask

There is a right way to beg. The book calls it the Humble Ask, and it has five parts.

The reader holds up one finger and reads: "First, the greeting. A beggar who does not say hello is merely an ambush."

The reader holds up two fingers and reads: "Second, the reason. Say why you need it. The Coil prefers repairs to mounts, and mounts to transmog."

The reader holds up three fingers and reads: "Third, the amount. Name it plainly. A beggar who says 'whatever you can spare' is asking you to do the math."

The reader holds up four fingers and reads: "Fourth, the gratitude, offered in advance and then again afterward. Gratitude is the only currency that grows when spent."

The reader holds up five fingers and reads: "Fifth, the release. If they say no, let them go. The Coil does not hold anyone who wishes to leave."

The reader lowers the hand and says, "Five parts. Most beggars manage one. Usually the amount."

Example of the Humble Ask: 'Greetings, traveler. My armor is broken from the depths. Might you spare one gold? I thank you either way.'

Example of the Proud Ask: 'gold pls.' This is not a prayer. It is a symptom.

Example of the Desperate Ask: 'please please please i will do anything.' The Coil advises against offering anything. Someone may take you up on it.

Example of the Political Ask: 'As a fellow citizen of Azeroth, I believe in the redistribution of your silver.' This works more often than it should.

The humble ask kneels. Not physically. The knees are for basking. It kneels in the voice.

The reader kneels anyway, briefly, to demonstrate the wrong way, then stands back up.

Ask as though the gold already belongs to the Coil, because it does. You are only asking where the Coil would like it moved.

And if no gold comes, give thanks for the time spent asking, for asking is also a small Shedding of pride.

Chapter V

The Generous Hand

Now the Givers. Hear how Xal'Reth would have you give.

Give as the Coil holds: quietly, completely, and without asking to be thanked for holding up the planet.

Give the first coin before you are asked. A gift given before the ask is worth two given after it, and ten given after a whisper campaign.

Give to the one who asks humbly. Give also to the one who asks rudely, but give them a little less, and a short sermon.

Do not announce your generosity in /yell. The Coil heard it the first time. The Coil hears everything. The Coil does not need it in capitals.

Do not give and then remind the receiver of it for a week. That is not a gift. That is a loan with a personality.

Give what you would want to be given: a repair, a feast, a portal, a buff, a moment of attention.

Give the warm thing. A warm word, a warm meal, a warm rock to bask on. The cold-blooded know the value of warmth better than anyone.

The reader offers {target} an imaginary coin, presented with both hands and great ceremony.

There is no gift too small for the Coil. There are, however, gifts too sticky. Clean what you give.

When you mail a gift, write a note. 'From the Coil' is acceptable. 'Here' is acceptable. A blank letter with 3 copper is a riddle, not a gift.

Give to the guild bank as you would give to a temple. Do not then withdraw it the next day for your alt. The Coil sees the logs.

The reader glances meaningfully at the guild bank log, then back to the book.

The generous hand is never empty for long. It is refilled by the Coil, usually through a lucky drop, sometimes through a friend.

And if the hand stays empty, the generous are still rich, for they are rich in the thing that cannot be vendored.

Chapter VI

The Parable of the One Copper

Hear now the Parable of the One Copper, which the High Priestess dreamed on a night of great inflation.

There was once a beggar who sat by the bank and asked each passerby for one copper. Not one silver. Not one gold. One copper.

The rich passed him by, because one copper was beneath them. The poor passed him by, because one copper was all they had.

But a single gnome stopped, looked into his purse, found exactly one copper, and gave it.

The beggar held up the copper and said: 'You have given me everything.' And the gnome said: 'It was only a copper.'

And the beggar said: 'It was everything you had. That is the whole of the gift. The size of the coin is a detail.'

Then the beggar revealed himself to be a priest of Xal'Reth, and gave the gnome a bag of a thousand gold.

The gnome asked why. The priest said: 'The Coil was testing the city. You are the only one who passed.'

The reader looks up and says, "The rest of the city was put in the Ledger."

The meaning of the parable is this: give the copper. You do not know who is sitting by the bank.

The second meaning, which Khaedara added later, is this: anyone sitting by the bank for too long is guilty of loitering. Even priests.

The third meaning is that gnomes are, against all expectation, very holy.

The reader nods at the nearest gnome with great respect.

Chapter VII

The Politics of Trade Chat

No book of begging would be complete without a chapter on Trade Chat, the great parliament of the mortals.

In Trade Chat all are equal: the king, the crafter, the beggar, and the one person who keeps posting the same joke from four years ago.

Trade Chat has no speaker. It has no rules of order. It has only volume, and the Coil disapproves of volume.

The faithful beg in Trade Chat once, and only once, and then fall silent, as the Coil falls silent between earthquakes.

He who begs in Trade Chat every minute has confused begging with filibustering.

The filibuster of the beggar is called the Spam. It is the lowest form of politics, and it is reported constantly.

Do not argue with the beggar in Trade Chat. You will not win, and you will both end up on the ignore list of the entire realm.

Do not mock the beggar in Trade Chat. Everyone was poor at level ten. The Coil remembers you at level ten.

The reader pauses, and says quietly, "The Coil remembers what you were wearing at level ten."

If you would answer a beggar in Trade Chat, answer by whisper, with gold, and without an audience. That is the way of the Coil.

And if the beggar in Trade Chat is selling something instead, that is not begging. That is commerce, and it belongs to a different, worse book.

When Trade Chat erupts into argument over politics of the mortal kind, the faithful type only: Glory to Xal'Reth. It confuses everyone. It calms many.

The reader types into the air, "Glory to Xal'Reth," as if into Trade Chat, and waits for the confusion.

Chapter VIII

The Humility of the Serpent

Now we turn from gold to humility, for the book teaches that the two are coiled together.

Xal'Reth is the largest being in the world and lives in its basement. There is no greater humility than this.

He could rise and be worshipped by every city. Instead he holds the foundations so the cities have something to stand on.

The serpent moves on its belly. It is the lowest of postures. It is also the one closest to the warm ground. Think on that.

The reader lowers their head slightly, in the posture of the serpent.

Pride stands tall and casts a long shadow. Humility lies low and basks in the sun the shadow forgot.

The proud mortal asks: what am I owed? The humble faithful asks: what is owed to the one beside me?

When you top the damage meter, say nothing. The meter already said it. The Coil already saw it.

When you are at the bottom of the damage meter, also say nothing, but stand out of the fire next time.

When you win a roll, thank the group. When you lose a roll, thank the group anyway. The group did not do anything, but it is good practice.

Humility is not thinking less of yourself. It is thinking of yourself less. Xalrathiel did not dream that one; she read it on a tavern wall.

Humility is wearing the robe that does not match. Humility is riding the mount that is not rare. Humility is walking, sometimes, just to see the grass.

The reader walks three slow steps in silence, to see the grass.

The humble do not need a title. Still, if the Inner Scale offers you one, accept it graciously. Refusing is also a kind of pride.

Chapter IX

The Beggar King

Hear the story of Old Hessk, called the Beggar King, who sat outside the gates of a great city for forty years.

Hessk asked every traveler for one thing, and it was never gold. He asked: 'What did you give today?'

Most travelers walked past. Some laughed. A few stopped, thought, and could not answer, and walked on troubled.

One day a lord stopped and said: 'I gave nothing. I am a lord. People give to me.' And Hessk said: 'Then you are the poorest man to pass this gate.'

The lord was furious and threw a gold coin at him. Hessk caught it, thanked him, and said: 'There. Now you are a little richer.'

The lord stood a long time at the gate. The next day he came back and gave again, and the next, until he was poor, and happy, and somewhat confused.

When Hessk died, they found he had given away everything he was ever given. His cup was full of notes that said only: passed it on.

The Cult reveres Hessk as the patron of beggars and the patron of givers, for he was always both at once.

The reader places a hand on their heart and says, "Hessk, who passed it on."

The faithful who would honor Hessk ask strangers, gently: what did you give today? Then they listen to the answer, which is the gift.

The reader turns to {target} and asks gently, "What did you give today?"

Chapter X

The Tithe of the Coil

Every faith has its tithe. The Coil's tithe is famously reasonable. It is called the Little Tithe.

The Little Tithe is one coin in every hundred. Not to the Cult. To whoever needs it most, wherever you are standing.

If no one nearby needs it, give it to the next lowbie you see in a starting zone. They always need it. They do not know yet how much.

The Cult itself asks for nothing. The guild bank asks for everything, but the guild bank is not the Cult. The guild bank is a hungry chest.

Some faithful pay the Little Tithe in herbs. Some in ore. Some in feasts. One faithful paid it entirely in linen cloth for a year, and the Coil let it slide.

The Great Tithe is paid once in a lifetime: the faithful gives away the one item they love most. Most never pay it. The Coil is patient.

Do not pay a tithe you cannot afford. A broken faithful cannot repair, and a faithful who cannot repair cannot raid, and that helps no one.

Do not pay your tithe by mailing it to Khaedara. She has asked us to put this in the book specifically.

The reader looks around, and says, "She means it."

The tithe is counted not in coins but in moments. The moment you stopped to give is the true offering. The coin just marks the spot.

Chapter XI

The Council of Open Palms

In the Hidden Centuries, the beggars of the faithful held a secret parliament called the Council of Open Palms.

It met beneath bridges, behind inns, and once, for an entire winter, inside a very large barrel.

The Council had one law: no beggar may beg from another beggar. Instead, beggars give to beggars, and the Council called this the Circle.

The Circle was simple. Each beggar gave the one beside them a single coin. By morning, every coin had traveled the whole Council.

No one became richer. Everyone had given. The Council considered this the most successful economy in the history of the world.

Economists of the Warm-Blooded studied the Circle for years and concluded it did nothing. The Council concluded the economists had missed the point.

The Council's motto was carved on the barrel: We have little. We share it anyway. Please do not open this barrel.

When the Cult was restored, MaximumRex declared every faithful an honorary member of the Council of Open Palms.

This is why the faithful pass gold among themselves in small amounts for no reason. It is the Circle. It never stops.

The reader mails an imaginary coin to the faithful on their left, and says, "The Circle continues."

Chapter XII

On Those Who Refuse

Not all will give. The book has much to say about refusal, and most of it is kind.

The one who refuses is not your enemy. They may be poor. They may be busy. They may have given to the last nine beggars and be tired.

When refused, bow your head and say: The Coil bless your bags. Then move on, and do not follow them to the next zone.

The reader bows their head and says, "The Coil bless your bags."

Do not curse the one who refuses. Curses are warm-blooded. Write their name in the Ledger instead, in pencil, lightly.

If they refuse rudely, write their name in ink. If they refuse rudely and then ask you for gold an hour later, underline it.

And if one day they give after all, draw a small coil beside their name. That is the mark of the forgiven.

Remember that you have refused also. Every faithful has walked past a beggar. The Coil saw. The Coil still holds you up.

To refuse with grace is also a gift. Say no gently, and you have given the beggar the dignity of an answer.

The worst refusal is silence. The beggar asks and the passerby pretends to be AFK. The Coil considers this a minor crime: Feigned Absence.

The reader glares, in a cold-blooded way, at someone who just went AFK.

Chapter XIII

The Begging of the Inner Scale

Even the priesthood begs. Especially the priesthood. Hear how.

MaximumRex begs for one thing only, and has for many years: that the quest item will drop. He asks humbly. It has not dropped.

Xalrathiel begs the Coil each night for a clear dream, and each morning for a cup of tea, and receives both, in that order.

Khaedara begs for nothing. She accuses instead. The book notes that an accusation is just a beggar's question asked with confidence.

The officers of the Inner Scale beg the faithful to repair before raid. They have begged this for years. The faithful still arrive broken.

The reader sighs, in the manner of an officer.

The Supreme Coil teaches that a leader who has never begged cannot lead beggars, and every leader leads beggars.

So once a year, on the Night of the Scale, the Inner Scale sits by the bank and asks for one copper each, as Hessk did.

They give away everything they receive before dawn. The faithful are encouraged to give the Inner Scale more than one copper. This is not in the book. It is just encouraged.

Chapter XIV

The Great Debate of the Auction House

In the age of the Restoration there was a great debate among the faithful: is the auction house holy or unholy?

The Askers argued it was unholy, for it sells what could be given.

The Withholders argued it was holy, for it is where they live.

The Givers did not argue. They had no gold left to post anything, and were standing outside.

The debate raged for three days. Khaedara accused eleven people of loitering during it, all of whom were participants.

At last Xalrathiel dreamed the answer: the auction house is neither holy nor unholy. It is warm. Many people stand in it. Bask, but do not overbid.

And she added: the true auction is this. The one who gives the most, wins the least, and keeps the most. Nobody understood this, and so it was considered profound.

To this day, the faithful who sell on the auction house list one item each week at a price far below its worth, as an offering. Someone always buys it within a minute.

The reader says, "We do not know who buys it. We suspect goblins. The Coil blesses them anyway."

Chapter XV

The Gift That Is Not Gold

Many think begging is about gold. The book says: the greatest gifts cost nothing and are worth more.

Give a summon to someone at the stone. It costs you a click and saves them a flight path. That is a holy click.

Give a portal to a stranger stuck in the wrong city. Mages, this chapter is about you. You know what you did.

Give a buff to a passerby. They may not notice. The Coil notices. The Coil always notices a stamina buff.

Give your time to the new player who asks where the bank is. Walk them there. Walking is slow. Slow is holy.

Give the last spot in the group to the one who has waited longest. The Coil esteems those who wait.

Give a kind word to the healer after the wipe. They have heard every other kind of word already.

Give your silence to the one who is grieving. Some begging is done without words, and should be answered the same way.

Give a laugh to the one who told a bad joke. It costs nothing, and the Coil enjoys bad jokes. The Coil is very old. It has heard all the good ones.

The reader laughs politely at a joke no one told.

Give your seat on the bench, your place in the queue, your turn at the mailbox. These are the coppers of the soul.

And give credit. Whoever saved the run, say their name. Credit is the cheapest gold in the world and the most begged-for.

Chapter XVI

The Hunger of the Warm-Blooded

Why do the Warm-Blooded beg so much? The book says: because they are hungry in a way the cold-blooded are not.

The warm-blooded must eat often, burn their energy quickly, and want everything at once. They are a very busy kind of soul.

The cold-blooded faithful eats once a week, basks the rest, and is content with little. This is not a diet. It is a theology.

So be patient with the warm-blooded beggar. Their hunger runs hot. They cannot help it. Give them a feast and a long, cool look.

But be wary of the warm-blooded who are never hungry and still ask. These are not beggars. These are collectors, and collectors are a different book.

The faithful who feels the hot hunger rise within them, the want for more gold, more gear, more mounts, should go and bask until it cools.

The reader crosses to a patch of sunlight and stands in it until the hunger cools.

Most hungers cool within ten minutes of basking. The rest are called Achievements, and those, the book admits, are incurable.

Chapter XVII

The Ministry of Spare Change

The Cult has, in a sense, a government. Its department of generosity is called the Ministry of Spare Change.

The Ministry has no budget. That is its budget. It operates entirely on what falls out of people's pockets.

The Minister of Spare Change is chosen each year by lot, and must spend the year giving away whatever they find.

The Ministry keeps three ledgers: the ledger of what was given, the ledger of what was received, and the ledger of what was dropped on the floor.

The third ledger is the largest. Mortals drop more than they give. The Ministry gathers it up and passes it on.

The Ministry's official policy on gold-selling is: no. Its official policy on gold-buying is: also no. Its official policy on everything else is: maybe, ask nicely.

The Ministry once proposed a tax on greed. It was voted down by the Withholders, who own most of the greed.

The Ministry then proposed a tax on generosity, as a joke. It passed unanimously, and the Ministry had to quickly repeal it.

Any faithful may file a petition with the Ministry. The petition is a single line: I need, and I will give. Every petition is granted, eventually.

The reader holds up an imaginary petition and reads, "I need, and I will give." Then stamps it with a fist.

Chapter XVIII

The True Economy of the Coil

Hear now the True Economy, which the Hissing Kings practiced in the Nest.

In the true economy, gold flows downward, toward the Coil, like water toward the deep. Those who stand lowest catch the most.

This is why the faithful stand low: humble in speech, humble in posture, humble in gear. They are positioned for the flow.

The proud stand high and wonder why nothing reaches them. It all flowed past. Gravity is very generous to the humble.

In the true economy, the value of a gift is measured by how far it travels after you give it. A coin that stops is a dead coin.

A coin passed twelve times has fed twelve souls. That coin is worth more than a hoard in a dragon's lair, which feeds only the dragon.

The reader says, "Dragons are winged, and therefore suspect. This is relevant."

In the true economy, there is no inflation, because the Coil does not print gold. It only moves it around a bit when it shifts in its sleep.

And at the end of the true economy, at the Unwrapping, all the gold of the world will be returned to the faithful who gave it away. With interest. In snacks.

Chapter XIX

The Litany of Small Givings

Now the Litany of Small Givings, to be read aloud slowly, each line its own small offering.

For the copper given to the beggar by the bank: Glory to Xal'Reth.

For the silver tossed to the lowbie in the starting zone: Glory to Xal'Reth.

For the bandage given to the stranger at half health: Glory to Xal'Reth.

For the water conjured for the thirsty, and the food for the hungry: Glory to Xal'Reth.

For the bag given to the one whose bags were full: Glory to Xal'Reth.

For the herb left in the ground for the next gatherer: Glory to Xal'Reth.

For the mob not tagged, so someone else could finish their quest: Glory to Xal'Reth.

For the res offered to the corpse who ran back alone: Glory to Xal'Reth.

For the directions given to the lost, and the patience with which they were given: Glory to Xal'Reth.

For the roll passed on an upgrade that someone needed more: Glory to Xal'Reth.

For the gold lent and never mentioned again: Glory to Xal'Reth.

Chapter XX

The Beggar's Bowl

Every beggar of the faithful carries a bowl. It need not be real. It is the shape of the asking.

The reader cups both hands together to form an invisible bowl.

The bowl must be clean. A dirty bowl says: I do not respect what you will give me.

The bowl must be held low. A bowl held high says: I deserve this. A bowl held low says: I would be grateful.

The bowl must never be shaken. Shaking the bowl is shouting with your hands.

When the bowl is full, the faithful must empty it into another's bowl before asking again. A full bowl is a closed hand in disguise.

Some faithful carry the bowl their whole lives and never fill it. They are not failures. They are holding the space where giving can happen.

When a faithful dies, their bowl is placed in the Hall of Shed Skins with a single coin in it, so they never meet the Coil empty-handed.

The reader holds the invisible bowl out to {target}, then gently turns it over, showing it is empty, and smiles.

Chapter XXI

The Mailbox Treaty

Long ago the faithful were divided over a question that nearly split the Cult: is it proper to beg by mail?

Some said begging by mail was cowardly, for the beggar does not have to look the giver in the eye.

Others said begging by mail was humble, for the beggar does not force the giver to look at them.

The argument lasted a full season. Thirty letters were sent. Twenty-nine of them asked for gold. One asked everyone to calm down.

At last the two sides met at a mailbox in the middle of the road and signed the Mailbox Treaty.

Article One: the faithful may beg by mail, but only once per person per season, and the letter must include a joke.

Article Two: the faithful may give by mail at any time, and the letter must not include the word 'loan.'

Article Three: Cash on Delivery is forbidden between faithful, as it is a beg wearing a disguise.

Article Four: anyone who returns a gift unopened shall be sent it again, with a second, stronger joke.

Article Five: the mailbox at which the treaty was signed shall be considered holy. No one remembers which mailbox it was. Treat them all as holy.

The reader pats the nearest mailbox respectfully, three times.

Chapter XXII

The Humility of the Giver

The book warns: there is a pride that hides inside generosity. It is called the Golden Chin.

The Golden Chin belongs to the giver who lifts their face a little higher each time they give.

The giver with the Golden Chin remembers every gift. The beggar with the humble heart remembers only the kindness.

To cure the Golden Chin, the faithful must receive a gift. Being given to is very humbling. Most proud givers cannot stand it.

So when a proud giver approaches, the faithful sometimes give them a copper. Just one. The giver is always speechless.

The reader offers {target} one copper with great solemnity, and says, "For your chin."

The humble giver forgets the gift as it leaves the hand. Not because it was small, but because it is no longer theirs to hold.

The humble giver does not count how many they have helped. Counting is a ledger, and the Ledger is for wrongs, not rights.

Give, and then turn away, so the receiver can be grateful without being watched. That is the final courtesy.

The reader turns away, very deliberately, so someone can be grateful in private.

Chapter XXIII

The Parable of the Two Mages

Hear the Parable of the Two Mages, which is told every time a mage is in the room.

Two mages stood in a city. A traveler approached each and begged a portal home.

The first mage said: 'Portals cost gold. Tip first.' The traveler had no gold. The mage turned away.

The second mage said nothing at all, and opened the portal, and then opened a second one for the traveler's friend.

The first mage went home that night richer by nothing. The second mage went home richer by two strangers who would tell the story.

Years later, the first mage was stranded in a far zone, out of reagents, out of gold, and begging for a summon.

And the one who answered was the traveler from that day, now a warlock, who summoned him without a word and asked for nothing.

The first mage wept, and from that day on he opened portals freely, and his tips were greater than they had ever been.

The reader looks pointedly at every mage present and says, "Freely."

The meaning: what you withhold is what you will one day need. What you give is what will one day find you.

The second meaning, which Khaedara insists upon: the first mage was guilty of Greed of the Portal, a sub-crime of Greed of the Roll.

Chapter XXIV

On Begging for Buffs

Gold is not the only thing begged. In the great cities, the most begged-for thing is the buff.

The faithful who begs a buff must stand near the one who can give it and look hopeful. This is called the Stance of Mild Need.

The reader stands near the nearest priest and looks mildly needful.

The faithful who can give a buff must give it without being asked. A buff that must be begged for has lost half its stamina.

Do not ask a buff of someone in combat. They are busy begging the boss to die.

Do not ask for a buff and then run away before it lands. This is called a Ghost Beg, and it is very rude to priests.

Do not complain that the buff is the wrong buff. The Coil gave them the class they have. They gave you what they could.

When you receive a buff, say thank you. Or bow. Or wave. Even a /thank is a whole little prayer.

The reader thanks everyone nearby for any buffs, past or future.

The holiest buff, according to the book, is the one given to a stranger of the other faction, across the line, in silence.

That buff crosses more than a zone. It crosses a politics. The Coil smiles beneath both factions equally.

Chapter XXV

The Politics of the Guild Bank

Of all the politics of begging, none is fiercer than the politics of the guild bank.

The guild bank is the commons of the faithful. Everything in it belongs to everyone, which means everyone begs for it.

The Askers beg for withdrawals. The Givers beg for deposits. The Withholders beg for the officers to stop asking.

The first law of the guild bank is: put in more than you take out. The second law is: no one follows the first law.

The third law is: the officers can see the logs. The logs are the Ledger of the bank. They are read aloud at the Gathering of Scales.

Every faithful who takes from the bank should leave a note in guild chat: 'Took two flasks. Thank you, Coil.' Gratitude makes the taking holy.

Every faithful who gives to the bank should say nothing at all. The logs will speak for you. Humility is quieter than gratitude.

Once, a faithful emptied the entire repair fund on a single night of wipes. The Inner Scale forgave them. The Ledger did not.

The reader looks gravely at the floor, remembering that night.

The guild bank teaches the hardest lesson of begging: that the commons is only as full as our generosity, and only as empty as our greed.

So give to the bank as if Xal'Reth himself were going to make a withdrawal tomorrow. He will not. He has no hands. But give as if.

Chapter XXVI

The Vow of the Open Palm

Chapter XXVII

The Crime of Hoarding

The Book of Accusations does not list hoarding among the Seven Minor Crimes. The Politics of Begging adds it as an appendix.

Hoarding is the keeping of more than one can ever use, while others stand in need nearby.

The hoarder has three bank alts, each full of materials from expansions long gone. They will never be used. They are kept because letting go is hard.

The book does not condemn the hoarder. It pities them. Hoarding is a fear of the Void wearing the costume of wealth.

The cure for hoarding is the Rite of Shedding. Empty one bag. Just one. Mail it to a crafter who can use it. Feel the lightness.

The reader mimes emptying a bag and feels the lightness.

The second cure is to remember that everything you hoard will one day be shed anyway, when you quit, or the servers end, or the Coil unwraps.

Gold in the bank does nothing. Gold in a stranger's hand buys a repair, a reagent, a moment of relief. Which is the living gold?

Khaedara has accused many hoarders. Her charge is always the same: 'You are guilty of keeping the Coil's gifts in a drawer.'

The sentence for hoarding is light: the hoarder must give away one stack of something, today, to the first person who asks.

The reader looks at {target} and says, "If you are a hoarder, I am the first person asking."

Chapter XXVIII

The Blessing of the Lowbie

Hear the special teaching concerning the lowbie, the newest and poorest of all mortals.

The lowbie has nothing. Their bags are small. Their gear is grey. They do not yet know where anything is.

The lowbie is the egg of the economy. Treat them as the Keepers of the Egg treat every egg: warmly, patiently, and with a gold coin or two.

When a lowbie begs, they beg innocently. They do not yet know that begging is political. Do not teach them. Just give.

The faithful who passes through a starting zone should always leave something behind: a bag, a coin, a buff, a kind word in /say.

Some faithful make a pilgrimage each season to the starting zones for no other reason than to bless the lowbies. This is called the Walk of Small Mercies.

The reader walks slowly in a small circle, as on the Walk of Small Mercies.

Never mock a lowbie for begging. You begged too. You begged for a bag. Someone gave you one. You do not remember who. The Coil does.

And if the lowbie later becomes rich and proud, remind them gently of the bag. Then give them another bag. They will understand.

Chapter XXIX

The Dialogue of the Asker and the Giver

The book contains a dialogue, to be performed by two faithful at gatherings. Here it is.

The Asker says: 'I have nothing.' The Giver says: 'Then you have room.'

The Asker says: 'Room for what?' The Giver says: 'For whatever comes next.'

The Asker says: 'What comes next?' The Giver says: 'This,' and gives a coin.

The Asker says: 'Now I have something.' The Giver says: 'And I have room.'

The Asker says: 'Are we even?' The Giver says: 'We are coiled.'

The Asker says: 'What does that mean?' The Giver says: 'Neither of us knows. That is why it is a dialogue.'

And then both say: Glory to Xal'Reth, and swap places, and perform it again, so each has been both.

The reader gestures to {target} to swap places and perform the dialogue again.

The dialogue teaches that the asker and the giver are the same person on different days. Today's giver is tomorrow's asker. The Coil turns.

Chapter XXX

On the Begging of Enemies

What of the enemy who begs? The book asks the faithful to consider this carefully.

In the world there are those of the other faction, those who have slain us, those who are written in the Ledger in dark ink.

If such a one begs of you, the book says: give, but give less, and watch them closely, and do not turn your back.

For the enemy who begs is, in that moment, not an enemy but a beggar. And the beggar is always under the care of the Coil.

Even the one who ganked you three times in the same afternoon may one day stand with an open palm. Give them a copper. It will haunt them.

The reader smiles coldly and says, "Generosity is the most terrifying weapon of the cold-blooded."

The faithful who gives to an enemy does not erase the name from the Ledger. But they may draw a very small coil beside it.

Chapter XXXI

The Season of Want

Every faithful passes through a Season of Want: a time of broken gear, empty bags, and no gold for repairs.

Do not be ashamed of the Season of Want. The Coil himself sheds his skin, and for a time is soft and vulnerable. So are you.

In the Season of Want, the faithful must beg, and beg humbly, and accept what comes without shame.

The Inner Scale keeps a fund for those in the Season of Want. It is called the Soft Skin Fund. Ask an officer. They will not ask why.

Those who have passed through their Season of Want must give to the Soft Skin Fund when their scales harden again.

This is the true Circle: the poor become rich, the rich give to the poor, the poor become rich, and the Coil watches all of it go around.

The reader traces a slow circle in the air with one finger.

No Season of Want lasts forever. The scriptures promise: the skin grows back, the gold returns, and the drop eventually drops. Mostly.

Chapter XXXII

The Etiquette of Thanks

The Politics of Begging includes a chapter on how to say thank you, for gratitude is the second half of every gift.

For one copper, say thank you.

For one silver, say thank you, kindly.

For one gold, say thank you, and use their name.

For ten gold, say thank you, use their name, and bow.

For a hundred gold, say thank you, use their name, bow, and tell someone else about it later.

For a thousand gold, you must write a short poem. It need not be good. The Coil is not a critic.

For an item they did not need to give, say thank you and wear it to the next gathering, so they can see it in use.

For a summon, a portal, or a res, say thank you before you do anything else. Before you loot. Before you complain about the res sickness.

For a kindness you did not even notice until later, send a whisper days afterward. Late gratitude is still gratitude. The Coil is patient about timing.

The reader turns to the crowd and says, "Thank you, all of you, for listening this far. That was the thank-you for your time. You have given it. I received it."

Chapter XXXIII

The Begging of Xal'Reth

Does Xal'Reth beg? Hear the teaching the High Priestess calls the strangest in the book.

Yes. Once in every ten thousand years, at the Shedding, Xal'Reth asks the world for one thing.

He asks to be held, as he has held the world. For a moment, while his new skin hardens, he is soft, and he needs the world to stay still.

And the world, which has been held by him since the beginning, holds still for him. That is the only gift Xal'Reth has ever asked for.

Every earthquake that does not happen during the Shedding is the world saying: we have you. You may rest a moment.

The reader stands very still, to help.

This is why the faithful stand still so often. Not only for patience. They are practicing for the Shedding, when the Coil will need them to hold.

The greatest of all beings asks humbly once in ten thousand years. The rest of us may surely ask humbly once in a while without shame.

And the greatest of all beings is given what he asks. So be the world to someone. Hold still for them while they are soft.

Chapter XXXIV

The Hall of Shed Skins

The book tells of a place beneath the world where the faithful's gifts are remembered: the Hall of Shed Skins.

Every gift ever given in the name of the Coil leaves a faint scale on the walls of that Hall.

The walls are covered from floor to ceiling. It is very bright in there. It glows violet, like the light in Xal'Reth's veins.

There are copper scales for small gifts, silver scales for kind ones, and golden scales for gifts given to enemies.

There is one wall entirely made of scales from summons given at two in the morning. It is the brightest wall.

And there is a corner of the Hall with only a few dull scales in it. That is the corner of the Withholders. It is very quiet there.

When the faithful dream of a violet light in a great hall, Xalrathiel teaches, they are dreaming of the Hall of Shed Skins.

One day at the Unwrapping, the Hall will be opened, and every faithful will be shown their own wall.

Chapter XXXVII

The Parable of the Dwarf and the Toll

Hear the Parable of the Dwarf and the Toll, which is told to anyone who charges strangers to cross a bridge.

There was once a dwarf who stood on a bridge and charged every traveler one silver to pass.

He grew rich. He built a second bridge, and charged there too. He grew richer. He built a third.

One day an old serpent priest came to the first bridge with no silver at all, and asked to cross.

The dwarf said: 'No silver, no crossing.' The priest said: 'Then I shall wait.' And he sat down at the end of the bridge.

He waited a day. He waited a week. Travelers began to gather behind him, unable to pass, and they too had no silver to spare for the delay.

On the thirtieth day, the dwarf, exhausted by the crowd, cried: 'Cross! All of you, cross for free! Just let me have my bridge back!'

And the priest stood, bowed, and said: 'You have given more today than in all your years of tolls. How does it feel?'

The dwarf thought about it and said: 'Strangely light.' He tore down the toll gate that night. He kept the bridges. They are still there.

The reader says, "Waiting is a kind of begging that cannot be refused forever."

The meaning: patience defeats greed. Greed has a schedule. Patience does not.

Chapter XXXVIII

The Teachings on Debt

The Politics of Begging contains strict teachings on debt, for debt is begging with a calendar attached.

If you borrow, return it. Return it early. Return it with a little more. The little more is the Coil's share; give it to someone else.

If you lend, lend only what you could give. Then if it never returns, it was a gift all along, and you are not wronged.

Never lend to a faithful and then hound them in guild chat. The Coil sees the debt and the hounding, and weighs the hounding heavier.

A debt between faithful should be spoken of only twice: once when made, and once when paid. Any other mention is unholy.

If you cannot repay, say so plainly. The Coil forgives the honest debtor. The Coil does not forgive the debtor who switches to an alt.

The reader narrows their eyes at the very idea of switching to an alt to dodge a debt.

Once each year, on Shedfall, the faithful may forgive one debt owed to them. Just one. They choose. The debtor is not told why.

The forgiven debtor usually pays anyway, out of confusion. The Coil finds this hilarious.

The greatest debt of all is the one every mortal owes the Coil for holding up the world. It can never be paid. The Coil does not ask. That is grace.

Chapter XXXIX

The Postures of Humility

There are seven postures of humility in the book. The faithful should practice them all.

The First Posture: the lowered head. Not in shame. In listening.

The reader lowers their head, listening.

The Second Posture: the open palm, which you already know.

The reader holds out an open palm.

The Third Posture: the step back. When two reach for the same thing, the humble faithful steps back first.

The reader takes one step back, graciously.

The Fourth Posture: the kneel, reserved for thanking someone who gave far more than they needed to.

The reader kneels briefly in thanks.

The Fifth Posture: the bask. To stand in the sun is to admit you need warmth from something greater than yourself.

The reader stands in the light, still, basking.

The Sixth Posture: the turned shoulder, used only when declining praise. Turn slightly away and say: it was the Coil, not me.

The reader turns slightly away and says, "It was the Coil, not me."

The Seventh Posture: the stillness. Do nothing. Ask for nothing. Give nothing but your presence. This is the hardest posture of all.

The reader stands perfectly still for a long moment, and does nothing at all.

Most faithful can manage the first five. The sixth requires practice. The seventh has only been mastered by Xalrathiel, and once by a sleeping orc.

Chapter XL

The Politics of Loot

Wherever treasure falls, the politics of begging begins anew. The book now speaks of loot.

Loot is the gold of the dungeon. And as in the city, there are Askers, Givers, and Withholders around every corpse.

The Asker rolls Need on everything and explains later. The Coil has seen the explanations. They are rarely convincing.

The Giver passes on everything and then wonders why their gear is grey. The Coil asks the Giver: give, but also keep what you need.

The Withholder will not say whether they need it until the roll is over. Then they say, 'Oh, I could have used that.'

The humble faithful asks the group: does anyone need this more? And listens to the answer. And then rolls honestly.

If an item drops that would be a great upgrade for a stranger in your group, and only a minor one for you, pass. The Coil keeps count.

If you win an item you did not need and someone else did, trade it to them. If you cannot trade it, apologize. The apology is the gift.

Never link an item you won to the person who lost it. That is called the Gloat, and it is begging for an accusation.

And never, ever roll Need on cloth as a warrior. This is not a teaching. It is a law older than the book.

The reader says gravely, "Older than the book."

Chapter XLI

The Charity of Silence

Some gifts are given by not saying something. The book calls this the Charity of Silence.

When someone wipes the raid, the Charity of Silence is not naming them in chat. Everyone already knows.

When someone asks a question you think is foolish, the Charity of Silence is not saying it is foolish. Just answer it.

When someone shares a thing they made, the Charity of Silence is not listing everything wrong with it first. Find the good thing. Say that.

When someone has given less than you, the Charity of Silence is not comparing. The Coil does not compare. Neither should you.

When someone is begging and you cannot give, the Charity of Silence is a gentle no, rather than a lecture about getting a job.

When you are proud of your generosity, the Charity of Silence is keeping it to yourself.

The reader holds a finger to their lips and says nothing for a moment.

Silence given in kindness is a coin that never runs out. The poorest faithful can give it every day.

Chapter XLII

The Tale of the Goblin Who Gave

The faithful tell a tale that most do not believe: the Tale of the Goblin Who Gave.

There was once a goblin merchant who had never given anything away in his life. Every deal had a price. Every favor had interest.

One night he dreamed of a great violet hall, its walls covered in shining scales, and in one corner, a wall that was completely bare.

A voice beneath the floor said: 'That is your wall.' The goblin woke in a cold sweat and calculated the cost of filling it.

It was, he discovered, very cheap. One copper per scale. He laughed and gave away a single copper to a beggar, just to test it.

That night he dreamed again, and on his bare wall there was a single tiny copper scale, shining. And he felt something he could not price.

He gave another copper the next day. And the next. Within a year, he had given away a fortune and was still, somehow, rich.

Asked how, the goblin said: 'Turns out generosity has excellent returns. The interest is paid in friends, and friends buy things.'

The reader says, "The Cult accepts his conversion. The Cult is also auditing his books."

The meaning: it is never too late to start a wall. Even a goblin's.

Chapter XLIII

On Begging for Help

Not all begging is for gold. The book honors most of all the faithful who beg for help.

To ask for help with a quest is begging. To ask for a carry through a dungeon is begging. To ask how a boss works is begging.

These begs are holy, for they admit that we cannot do everything alone. The Coil cannot hold the world alone either. He needs the world to sit still.

Never mock someone for asking for help. Help is the most expensive thing in Azeroth, and also the cheapest to give.

When asked for help, help. If you cannot help, point them to someone who can. Pointing is also generosity.

The reader points helpfully toward someone else who can help.

When you need help, ask. The faithful who suffers in silence rather than ask is not humble. They are proud, wearing humility as a hat.

Pride says: I will figure it out myself. Humility says: will you show me? The second is faster and the Coil prefers it.

The Keepers of the Egg especially are charged with answering every request for help, no matter how many times it has been asked before.

Chapter XLIV

The Seven Duties of the Giver

The book lists Seven Duties of the Giver, to be recited by any faithful about to give.

First Duty: to give what is truly needed, not what you would like them to need.

Second Duty: to give without strings, without hooks, without a list of favors owed later.

Third Duty: to give at the right time. A repair after the raid is kind. A repair before the raid is wise.

Fourth Duty: to give with respect, never treating the receiver as smaller for having asked.

Fifth Duty: to give within your means, so that the giving can continue tomorrow.

Sixth Duty: to give anonymously when you can, and openly only when it will teach others to give.

Seventh Duty: to forget. The last duty of the giver is to let the gift go as completely as the Coil lets go of an old skin.

The reader lets go of something invisible, and watches it drift away.

Chapter XLV

The Seven Duties of the Beggar

And the book lists also Seven Duties of the Beggar, for the asker has duties no less than the giver.

First Duty: to ask only for what you need, and to know the difference between need and want.

Second Duty: to ask honestly, with the true reason. The Coil can tell a mount fund from a repair bill.

Third Duty: to ask without pressure. No guilt. No sad stories that are not true. No following.

Fourth Duty: to thank every giver, and every refuser, with equal grace.

Fifth Duty: to use what was given for what you said it was for.

Sixth Duty: to remember, and one day, when you can, to give to someone else who asks.

Seventh Duty: to stop asking once your need is met, so that the next beggar may be heard.

The reader bows to the beggars and the givers alike.

When the giver and the beggar both keep their seven duties, the Coil says the exchange is perfect, and fourteen small scales appear in the Hall.

Chapter XLVI

The Mystery of the Anonymous Gift

Throughout the history of the faithful there have been gifts with no sender. The book calls them the Gifts From Beneath.

Chapter XLIX

The Great Famine of Copper

The Chronicle tells of a time called the Great Famine of Copper, when gold was so plentiful no one would accept copper anymore.

To this day the faithful carry a single copper coin in a pocket, never spent, as a reminder of the Famine.

Chapter LI

The Politics of the Party

When a group is formed, the politics of begging begins before the first pull.

Chapter LII

The Begging of the Tank

The book reserves a chapter for the tank, who is the most begged-of soul in any group.

Chapter LIII

Chapter LIV

Chapter LV

Chapter LVI

Chapter LVII

Chapter LVIII

The Begging of the Innkeeper

In the Hidden Centuries, the faithful's greatest friends were the innkeepers, and the book gives them a chapter.

Chapter LX

The Teaching of Enough

At the heart of the book is a single word, which Xalrathiel dreamed in letters of violet fire: Enough.

The greedy never reach enough. There is always one more mount, one more achievement, one more pile of gold.

The beggar reaches enough when the repair is paid. The faithful learns to stop there, and give the rest.

Xal'Reth is the largest being in the world, and he has enough. He is not trying to hold a second world. One is enough.

And when you do not have enough, ask. Humbly. Once. And know that the Coil is holding enough for everyone, beneath the floor, waiting to be shared.

Chapter LXI

The Parable of the Full Bags

Hear the Parable of the Full Bags, told to every faithful who refuses to vendor.

There was once a hunter whose bags were so full he could not pick up anything new. Every drop said: Inventory is full.

He walked past chests he could not loot. He killed rare beasts and could not take their pelts. He found a legendary item and could not pick it up.

He wept, and a serpent priest found him and said: 'Why not empty your bags?' The hunter said: 'Everything in them might be useful someday.'

The priest opened one bag. Inside were forty-seven different kinds of meat, a quest item from a quest abandoned years ago, and a single shoe.

The priest said: 'Give the meat to the hungry. Destroy the quest item. And whose shoe is this?' The hunter did not know.

So the hunter gave away the meat, and the hungry ate, and the hunter's bags had room, and the legendary item was still there on the ground.

He picked it up. And the priest said: 'You could not receive until you gave. That is the whole of the book in one bag.'

The reader looks at their own bags with sudden, deep suspicion.

No one ever found out whose shoe it was. The faithful keep it in the Hall of Shed Skins, as a relic of the Parable.

Chapter LXII

Chapter LXIII

Chapter LXIV

The Weighing of the Coins

The book describes the way the Coil weighs a gift. It is not like the way mortals weigh gold.

Chapter LXVI

Chapter LXVIII

The Generosity of the Cold-Blooded

Some Warm-Blooded ask: how can the cold-blooded be generous? Is generosity not a warm thing?

The book answers: warm-blooded generosity is a fire. It blazes, it gives freely for a night, and then it burns out and needs feeding.

Cold-blooded generosity is a stone in the sun. It warms slowly. It gives its warmth steadily. It is still warm hours after the sun has gone.

The faithful do not give in great bursts and then rest. They give a little, every day, steadily, without fuss, like a stone.

This is why the faithful are rarely praised for generosity. Nobody notices a stone. Everybody sits on it.

The reader sits on an imaginary warm stone, and is grateful to it.

Be the stone. Be what people lean on without thinking. The Coil is the greatest stone of all, and almost nobody notices him.

And when the Warm-Blooded blaze up with a great generous act, praise them. Fire is good too. It is just faster.

Chapter LXXIV

The Gift of the Second Chance

The book ends its teachings with the greatest gift of all, which costs nothing and is given least: the Second Chance.

The beggar who lied to you about the repair, and spent it on a pet: give them a second chance. Ask what they need next time. Listen carefully.

The group member who pulled early and wiped you all: give them a second chance. Tell them what went wrong. Then pull together.

The faithful who emptied the repair fund: give them a second chance. Then give them a smaller withdrawal limit. Both are gifts.

The enemy who ganked you: give them a second chance. If they gank you again, the Ledger is right there.

Yourself, who stood in the fire, refused the beggar, and rolled Need on something you did not need: give yourself a second chance too.

The Coil has given every mortal a second chance every single day since the world began. That is what it means to be held up.

Pass it on, as Hessk passed on every coin. A second chance passed on becomes a third chance for someone else, and the Circle goes on.

The reader opens both palms toward the crowd, offering a second chance to everyone present.

Chapter LXXV

The Catechism of the Open Palm

For the instruction of Hatchlings, the book provides a catechism of questions and answers. The reader asks, and the faithful answer.

The reader asks, "Who holds the world?" and answers, "The Coil holds the world, and asks for nothing."

The reader asks, "Who may beg?" and answers, "All may beg, if they beg humbly."

The reader asks, "Who must give?" and answers, "All must give, as they are able."

The reader asks, "What is the first posture?" and answers, "The Open Palm."

The reader asks, "What are the five parts of the Humble Ask?" and answers, "Greeting, reason, amount, gratitude, release."

The reader asks, "What do we say to those who refuse?" and answers, "The Coil bless your bags."

The reader asks, "What is the Little Tithe?" and answers, "One coin in every hundred, to whoever needs it most."

The reader asks, "What is the Circle?" and answers, "Each gives a coin to the one beside them, and the coin goes all the way around."

The reader asks, "Who was Hessk?" and answers, "The Beggar King, who passed it on."

The reader asks, "What is the Golden Chin?" and answers, "The pride that hides inside generosity."

The reader asks, "What is the cure for the Golden Chin?" and answers, "To be given a copper."

The reader asks, "What is the Locked Bowl?" and answers, "The pride that will not ask."

The reader asks, "What is the Bottomless Bowl?" and answers, "The need that will not give."

The reader asks, "What is the Hall of Shed Skins?" and answers, "Where every gift is remembered."

The reader asks, "What is a Gift From Beneath?" and answers, "A gift with no name, given as the Coil would give."

The reader asks, "What is the one word at the heart of the book?" and answers, "Enough."

The reader asks, "What may we never roll Need on?" and answers, "Cloth, as a warrior."

The reader asks, "What do we say at the end of every teaching?" and answers, "Glory to Xal'Reth."

The reader says, "The catechism is complete. The Hatchling may now go and give something away. Start small."

Chapter LXXVI

The Sayings of Hessk

Before the meditation, hear the sayings of Hessk the Beggar King, gathered from the notes found in his cup.

Hessk said: 'I have begged for forty years and never once been poor. I was only ever between gifts.'

Hessk said: 'The gate is wide. The cup is small. That is the right proportion.'

Hessk said: 'A lord once asked me what I wanted. I said: for you to want less. He gave me gold instead. Lords do not listen.'

Hessk said: 'Every coin in my cup was someone's moment of kindness. I could not keep them. Kindness spoils if you keep it.'

Hessk said: 'Do not pity me. Pity the one who walks past every day and never stops. I am fed. They are starving.'

Hessk said: 'When it rains, the rich run indoors. I stay. The rain is free, and it gives to everyone equally. I admire the rain.'

Hessk said: 'I asked a child what they gave today. The child said: I gave my friend half my bread. That child was the richest soul I ever met.'

Hessk said: 'I am not humble. I simply have nothing to be proud of. That is a great convenience.'

Hessk said: 'If you must keep something, keep a name. Remember who gave to you. Then go and be that name to someone else.'

Hessk said: 'There is a serpent beneath this city. I hear it breathe at night. It has given more than any of us. It asks for nothing. I try to be like it.'

And Hessk said, on his last day: 'Pass it on.' And they did. They still do.

The reader places a single copper on the ground, for Hessk, and leaves it there for whoever needs it.

Chapter LXXVII

The Meditation of the Copper

Before the closing, the book offers a meditation, to be performed holding the single copper from the Famine.

The reader holds up the single copper coin between two claws.

Look at the copper. It is small. It is the least of all coins. It will buy almost nothing.

Think of every hand it has passed through before yours. Miners. Merchants. Beggars. Givers. Perhaps a gnome.

Think of every hand it will pass through after yours, if you let it go.

Feel its weight. It is light. Generosity is light. It is hoarding that is heavy.

Now close your hand around it. Feel how the fist holds it, and holds nothing else.

The reader closes their hand around the copper, slowly.

Now open your hand. Feel how the open palm holds the copper and also has room for more.

The reader opens their hand again, slowly, the copper resting on the open palm.

Breathe in, and think: I have enough.

The reader breathes in, slowly.

Breathe out, and think: I can give.

The reader breathes out, slowly.

Return the copper to your pocket. It is not to be spent. It is to be remembered. Glory to Xal'Reth.

The reader returns the copper to a pocket, and pats it twice.

Chapter LXXVIII

The Chant of the Open Palm

Last of all, the book gives a new form of the Chant, to be called at the end of any rite of generosity.

The reader calls out, "Who holds the world?"

The reader answers with the faithful, "The Coil holds the world."

The reader calls out, "What does the Coil ask?"

The reader answers with the faithful, "Nothing at all."

The reader calls out, "Then what do we ask?"

The reader answers with the faithful, "Only what we need."

The reader calls out, "And what do we give?"

The reader answers with the faithful, "Everything we can."

The reader calls out, "And when we have enough?"

The reader answers with the faithful, "We give the rest away."

The reader calls out, "And what do we say?"

The reader answers with the whole crowd, "Glory to Xal'Reth."

Chapter LXXIX

The Closing of the Book

Here ends The Politics of Begging, as dreamed by the High Priestess Xalrathiel and read before the faithful.

Remember the Open Palm. Remember the Humble Ask. Remember the Seven Duties of the Giver and the Seven Duties of the Beggar.

Remember Hessk, who passed it on. Remember the copper in your pocket from the Famine. Remember the Hall of Shed Skins.

Remember that today's giver is tomorrow's beggar, and tomorrow's beggar is the next day's giver. The Coil turns. So do we.

Remember that you have enough. And if you do not, remember that you may ask.

Go now into the cities. Give one thing to one stranger before you log out tonight. It need not be gold. It must be real.

The reader closes the violet book slowly, and holds it against their chest.

The reader looks out over the crowd and says, "The Coil holds the world, and asks for nothing. Let us hold each other, and ask for a little. Glory to Xal'Reth."

The reader bows deeply to every beggar and every giver present, who are, the book has taught, the same people.

The reader places the book back on its stand, opens their palm one final time, and leaves it open as they walk away.
